# Astronomy

Intro astronomy for orbits, light-year distances, galaxies, and redshift as a wavelength shift.

## Method

1. Sort the question into an orbit, a cosmic distance, or a galaxy-scale pattern.
2. For an orbit, name the central mass, the period T, and the semi-major axis a.
3. Apply Kepler's third law: T squared is proportional to a cubed for that same central mass.
4. Treat a light-year as distance only, the length light travels in one year.
5. Read redshift as a wavelength shift of a known line toward longer wavelength.
6. Keep a local orbit and a galaxy redshift on separate scales, and state which one you are using.
7. Give the relation and its assumption, and do not invent an observation.

## Formulas

- Kepler's third law: `T^2 ∝ a^3`. For one central mass, the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis.
- Kepler's third law (explicit): `T^2 = (4π^2/(G M)) a^3`. The proportionality constant is 4π squared divided by G times the central mass M.
- Light-year: `1 ly ≈ 9.46 × 10^12 km`. A light-year is a distance of about 9.46 trillion kilometers, not a unit of time.
- Redshift: `z = (λ_obs − λ_rest) / λ_rest`. Redshift is the fractional wavelength shift when the observed line is longer than the rest line.

## Checks

- A larger semi-major axis means a longer period, with T scaling as a to the power 3/2.
- A light-year answers how far, even though the name contains a year.
- Redshift z is positive only when the observed wavelength is longer than the rest wavelength.
- Do not use an orbit formula to size a galaxy or a redshift to replace a period.

## Limits

- These are intro relations, not a navigation solution or an observing schedule.
- The card does not choose between a motion redshift and cosmic expansion without the scale and the data.
- It stays on orbits, distances, galaxies, and redshift, and it is not a sky forecast.
