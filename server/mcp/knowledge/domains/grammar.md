# Grammar

**Scope:** Standard written-English patterns for parts of speech, clauses, agreement, tense, and modifier placement.

## Method

1. Name the part of speech of the word in question.
2. Mark each group as a clause or a phrase.
3. Match the finite verb to the grammatical subject.
4. Match each pronoun to one clear antecedent.
5. Keep tense consistent unless the time really changes.
6. Place each modifier next to the word it modifies.
7. Reread the syntax as one sentence, not as isolated words.

## Formulas

- **Parts of speech:** Noun, pronoun, verb, adjective, adverb, preposition, conjunction, and interjection. Label the job a word does in this sentence, not a job it can do in some other sentence.
- **Clause vs phrase:** A clause has a subject and a finite verb; a phrase does not. Call a group a clause only when both the subject and a finite verb are present.
- **Subject-verb agreement:** The verb agrees with its grammatical subject in number and person. Ignore a nearby noun in a prepositional phrase when choosing the verb.
- **Pronoun agreement:** A pronoun agrees with its antecedent in number and person. Singular they for a specific person is an accepted modern choice, not an automatic error.
- **Tense consistency:** Keep one time frame unless the meaning requires a shift. A shift is fine for a real time change, a universal fact, or quoted speech.
- **Modifier placement:** Place a modifier next to the word or phrase it modifies. An opening participial phrase should modify the subject of the main clause.
- **Participle:** A participle, such as walking or broken, modifies; it is not by itself a finite verb. Do not treat a participial phrase as a complete clause.
- **Syntax:** Syntax is the order that lets the parts of a sentence fit together. Fix order only after the subject, verb, and modifiers are identified.

## Checks

- A phrase was not treated as a complete clause.
- The verb agrees with the subject, not with the object or a noun in a phrase.
- Each pronoun has one clear antecedent and agrees with it.
- A participle or other modifier sits next to the word it modifies.

## Limits

- These are standard written-English patterns, not every dialect or creative choice.
- A deliberate break is not an error when it is marked as a choice.
- Grammar patterns do not decide what a word means.
